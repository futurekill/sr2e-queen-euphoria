# Plan: make the Queen Euphoria scenes look like real battle maps
_Round 1 — revised after Codex_

## Goal

The nine scenes currently ship a wireframe: correct dimensions, correct grid,
correct rooms and walls, flat dark fill with labels. That was scaffolding to lock
the geometry. futurekill wants them to look like actual battle maps — textured
floors, real walls, furniture, lighting — without losing anything the geometry
layer guarantees.

## The constraint that rules out the obvious approach

Straight AI image generation cannot do this. A battle map has to align to a 1 m
grid: a wall must sit exactly on a cell boundary, a 4.5 m room must be exactly
4.5 cells wide, and the art must agree with the wall segments Foundry uses for
line-of-sight. No diffusion model places geometry to the pixel, and "generate
then nudge" does not converge across nine maps at up to 2700 × 2000 px.

We also cannot reproduce the printed plans — the module's own copyright stance
forbids reproduced layout, which is why layouts are authored rather than traced.

## Prerequisite: four scenes have no geometry to paint

`layout` and `doors` exist for five scenes only. **Euphoria's Condo, Pacific
Towers Lobby and both Hive levels are perimeter-only**, so per-room materials,
furniture and aligned interior walls are unimplementable there without inventing
geometry — which is frozen. Those four layouts must be authored and signed off
*before* their art pass. Until then they keep the wireframe, which is honest: a
wireframe of a room list we have is better than textured art of a layout we made
up at render time.

Order follows the sign-off loop futurekill asked for anyway: author layout →
preview → sign off → art.

## Approach: composite, don't generate

Keep `layout` (room rectangles in metres) as the single authoritative source, and
render on top of it:

1. **Materials, not colours.** Each room declares a `material`
   (`concrete`, `carpet`, `tile`, `industrial`, `chitin`, `asphalt`, `soil`).
   Each material is **ONE seamless 512×512 base tile** generated by AI, stored in
   `assets/textures/`, tiled into the room rectangle. Nine maps share maybe eight
   tiles, so the art cost is bounded and reviewable — and a tile is not a layout,
   so nothing of the printed plan is reproduced.

   **One base, not several variants.** Independently-generated seamless tiles are
   each seamless against *themselves*, not against *each other* — variant A's
   right edge will not meet variant B's left. Mixing them would put a visible
   discontinuity at every tile boundary, which is worse than the repetition it
   was meant to cure. Variety comes instead from **boundary-safe overlays**
   composited on top: stains, wear, scuffs and debris placed from a seeded hash
   of position, at a frequency unrelated to the tile pitch, and never crossing a
   tile edge in a way that needs to match. (A deliberately edge-compatible
   Wang-tile set is the alternative, but it means testing every permitted
   adjacency, and one base plus overlays gets most of the benefit for far less
   work.)
2. **Walls with real thickness**, drawn from the same segments Foundry walls come
   from: a dark core with a lighter inner edge and a drop shadow onto the floor.

   **Doors render state-neutral** — jambs and a threshold, or at most a faint
   swing arc; never a leaf drawn open. The background is a static image and
   cannot change when Foundry opens or closes the door, and doors are created
   closed (`ds: 0`). An angled leaf would permanently depict one state while the
   actual door — and its line-of-sight — says the other. Foundry already shows
   door state with its own control icon; the art must not contradict it.
3. **Furniture as generated top-down prop sprites** placed per room in metres —
   bed, couch, desk, counter, toilet, vat, crate, cocoon. AI generates a small
   sprite sheet; the renderer positions and clips each from metre coordinates.
   Shaded SVG primitives were the first idea and are rejected: over a repeating
   tile they read as a diagram, and furniture needs real texture, small-scale
   detail and occlusion shadow to stop looking schematic. These are set dressing
   — nothing mechanical depends on them, and AI supplies decorative assets only,
   never structural geometry.
4. **Lighting pass**: a soft vignette, plus warm pools under the light sources a
   scene declares. The Hive Lower Level gets none — the book gives it no power —
   so it renders near-black with only the players' own light to come.
5. Compose in SVG, rasterise with `rsvg-convert` — **but not through stdin.**
   The current pipeline pipes SVG in on stdin, which gives librsvg no base URI,
   so a relative `<image href="assets/textures/…">` silently renders empty.
   Write the SVG to a temp file in the repo root so relative paths resolve, or
   embed each tile as a `data:` URI. Verified against the installed librsvg
   2.62.3 — this is a real failure, not a theoretical one.

6. **Draw walls from the punched SOLID segments only.** `walls()` returns solid
   segments *and* door Wall documents in one array. Painting everything it
   returns would stroke a solid wall straight through every doorway — the art
   would contradict the geometry it was derived from, and every door would look
   shut and impassable. Split the return, or have the renderer read the same
   `punch()` output the wall builder does.

## Why this stays correct

- Art and walls derive from the same rectangles, so they cannot drift.
- Everything is deterministic and re-runnable; no map depends on a lucky sample.
- Textures are reviewable in isolation before any map is built.
- If a texture is ugly we regenerate one 512×512 tile, not a 2700×2000 map.

## Key decisions & tradeoffs

1. **Tiled textures over whole-map generation.** Loses painterly uniqueness;
   gains exact alignment, determinism and a bounded art budget. For a *battle*
   map, alignment beats uniqueness — a beautiful map whose walls sit half a metre
   off is worse than a plain one that is correct.
2. **Generated prop sprites for furniture, procedural everything structural.**
   The deterministic layer keeps every wall, door and room edge; AI touches only
   decoration. Costs a sprite sheet to review, and buys the difference between a
   battle map and a floorplan diagram.
3. **Per-room material rather than per-map style.** More data to author, but the
   Hive's chitin-and-concrete contrast with the condo's carpet is most of what
   sells these as different places.

## Risks / open questions

1. ~~Does tiling read repetitive?~~ **Yes, and the first mitigation was wrong.**
   A 512 px tile at ~4 m repeats about 6.75× across a 27 m room, and rotating or
   offsetting *once per room* just relocates the pattern — it does nothing about
   repetition *within* the room. Resolution: **one base tile plus seeded
   boundary-safe overlay sprites** (stains, wear, scuffs, debris), placed from a
   hash of position at a frequency unrelated to the tile pitch. Multiple base
   variants were considered and rejected — see approach step 1: independently
   seamless tiles do not tile against *each other*, so mixing them replaces
   repetition with a visible discontinuity at every boundary. Seams get validated
   at the real 400 px display period, not at 512.
2. ~~Is SVG shading enough for furniture?~~ **No — resolved: generate top-down
   prop sprites.** A small vocabulary of clean shaded primitives over one
   repeating tile still reads as a diagram. Furniture needs texture, small-scale
   detail and occlusion shadows to stop looking schematic. So AI generates
   *decorative assets* — prop sprites and overlay/decal sprites — and never
   structural geometry; the props are clipped and positioned from metre
   coordinates like everything else.
3. ~~Ordering?~~ **One complete map first — Royal Meadows.** Its geometry is
   already signed off, and a vertical slice tests texture scale, clipping,
   shadows, furniture readability, webp compression and how it actually looks in
   Foundry all at once. Commissioning a full texture set before knowing whether
   the composite reads as a map is the expensive order.
4. Foundry performance with nine backgrounds up to 2700 × 2000 webp. (Codex
   confirmed librsvg itself handles raster patterns, clip paths and drop shadows
   at that size without memory concern — keep filter regions bounded so shadows
   are neither clipped nor needlessly expensive.)

## Out of scope

- Changing any dimension, room rectangle, wall or door. Geometry is settled.
- Token placement and encounter journals.
